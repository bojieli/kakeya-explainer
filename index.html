The Kakeya set problem: every direction, almost no volume.

Why this problem matters now

In July 2026, Hong Wang and Yu Deng became China’s first Fields Medal recipients. Wang’s official citation highlighted her major advances on the Kakeya problem in three dimensions. Her work with Joshua Zahl resolved the three-dimensional conjecture explained here.

At a finite scale, an ideal line segment looks like a thin tube. The puzzle is whether tubes pointing throughout space can overlap so efficiently that their volume becomes tiny—and whether that can make the underlying set less than three-dimensional.

Two measurements, two different questions

A small occupied fraction at one scale does not decide the dimension. What matters is how fast the required cube count grows over arbitrarily finer rulers.

What the theorem says

Every Kakeya set in ℝ3 has dimension 3.

Clever overlap may make the occupied fraction—and even ordinary volume—zero. But it cannot produce a lasting power-law saving in the number of tiny cubes required.

In plain language: the set can be extremely sparse, but it cannot behave like a surface, a curve, or anything with fewer than three dimensions.
